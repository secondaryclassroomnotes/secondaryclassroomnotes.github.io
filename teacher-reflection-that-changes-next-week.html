Teacher Reflection That Changes Next Week, Not Just the Journal

Teachers reflect constantly. The trick is making it change something.

By Clay Shumate3 min read

  2. What separates useful reflection from venting
  3. A five-minute weekly routine
  4. What reflections actually look like
  5. Common questions

Most teachers reflect constantly. On the drive home, in the shower, at 2 a.m. The problem isn't a shortage of reflection. It's that most of it doesn't change anything about the next lesson.

Useful reflection is short, specific and ends with a decision. Here's a simple structure for secondary teachers who want reflection that shows up in next week's plans instead of just a journal.

Look at the lesson from more than one angle

Stephen Brookfield's widely used model of critically reflective teaching suggests looking at your practice through four lenses. As summarized by UC Berkeley's Center for Teaching & Learning, they are your own experience, your students' eyes, your colleagues' experiences, and the research literature.1

Most teacher reflection stays in the first lens. It's the easiest, and it's also where our blind spots live. Adding even one more lens, usually the students', makes reflection noticeably more honest.

LensA quick way to use it
Your own experienceThree sentences after class: what worked, what didn't, what surprised you
Students' eyesA one-question exit slip: "What helped you most today? What confused you?"
ColleaguesAsk one colleague how they teach the same concept
ResearchCheck one claim you're relying on against an actual source

What separates useful reflection from venting

Venting is fine. Teachers need it. But it isn't reflection, and it's worth knowing which one you're doing.

  • Useful reflection names something specific. "The transition into groups took nine minutes" rather than "Fourth period was a mess."
  • It looks for a cause you control. Not "they were wild," but "the directions were only spoken, not posted."
  • It ends with one change. Not five. One, small enough to try tomorrow.
  • It checks back. Did the change work? That's where most reflection quietly stops.

A five-minute weekly routine

  1. Pick one class period. The one that's bothering you most, or the one that surprised you.
  2. Write what happened in two sentences. Facts only.
  3. Read the student exit slips from that period, if you have them. Look for a pattern, not the outliers.
  4. Choose one change for next week and write it on the plan itself, not in a separate notebook.
  5. Next week, start by checking whether the change happened and what it did.
Keep it where you'll see it

Reflection written in a journal you never reopen won't do much. Write the one change directly on next week's lesson plan, where you'll run into it.

What reflections actually look like

It helps to see real examples written out, especially the difference between a reflection that sounds thoughtful and one that changes practice. Elevate the Norm has a set of teacher reflection examples written for secondary classrooms, including what separates a useful reflection from a performative one.

Common questions

How often should teachers reflect?

A short weekly routine is more sustainable than daily journaling for most teachers. Consistency matters more than length.

Should reflection be shared with administrators?

That depends on your school. Private reflection tends to be more honest. If reflection is part of an evaluation, keep a separate private version for yourself.

What if student feedback is harsh?

Look for patterns rather than single comments. One harsh slip is noise. Five students saying the same thing is information worth acting on.
