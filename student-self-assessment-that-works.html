What a Student Self-Assessment Form Should Actually Ask

"How do you think you did?" gets you "good." Here's what to ask instead.

By Clay Shumate3 min read

Most student self-assessment forms ask some version of "How do you think you did?" and get some version of "Good." That isn't laziness. It's a question with no criteria attached, and a vague question gets a vague answer.

Self-assessment can be one of the most useful habits a middle or high school student builds, but only when the form asks the right things. Here's what the research says separates the forms that work from the ones that just fill a folder.

What the research actually supports

In a critical review of the research on student self-assessment, Heidi Andrade found a consistent positive association between formative self-assessment and learning, with gains in both achievement and self-regulation. She also found three conditions that matter most: students need clear, task-specific criteria; the self-assessment has to be formative, meaning students can act on it before the work is final; and feedback and modeling make it much more effective.1

The same review carries a warning worth taking seriously. When self-assessments count toward a grade, students' ratings tend to drift away from what an outside reader would say. Ask a teenager to grade themselves for points and you'll get a negotiation, not a reflection.

The short version

Give students criteria, let them use the assessment before the work is done, and don't grade the rating itself.

Five questions a strong form asks

  1. Which criterion did you meet best, and where's the proof? Pointing to a specific paragraph, problem or step forces students past "I tried hard."
  2. Which criterion is your work not meeting yet? The word yet matters. So does requiring an answer. "Nothing" isn't allowed.
  3. What rating would you give each criterion, and why? Use the same scale and wording as your rubric so the student and the teacher are measuring the same thing.
  4. What's one change you'll make before this is final? This turns the form from a record into a plan.
  5. What do you want your teacher to look at closely? Students often know exactly where their work is shaky. Ask them.

When to use it

Timing is the difference between a form that changes the work and one that just describes it.

Teaching students to rate honestly

Accuracy isn't automatic. It's a skill, and it improves with repetition and feedback. A few habits help:

  • Model it first. Rate a sample piece of work out loud against the rubric, including the parts that fall short.
  • Compare, don't correct. When your rating differs from theirs, ask them to explain their reasoning before you explain yours.
  • Keep it short. One page. A form that takes twenty minutes will get rushed.
  • Do it again. The first round is always the weakest. By the third, most students are noticeably sharper.

If you'd rather not build the form from scratch, Elevate the Norm has a free student self-assessment form that requires evidence for every rating. Students are expected to cite their work, not just circle a number.

Common questions

Should self-assessments be graded?

Grade completion if you need to, but not the rating itself. When the rating affects the grade, students have a reason to inflate it, and the research suggests that's exactly what happens.

What about students who always rate themselves low?

Treat it the same way as over-rating: ask for evidence. Students who underrate often can't see what they're doing well, and naming it specifically helps them calibrate.

Does self-assessment work for group projects?

Yes, and it's especially useful there. Have each student assess their own contribution individually before the group talks, so one confident voice doesn't set everyone's rating.
