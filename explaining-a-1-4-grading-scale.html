How to Explain a 1–4 Grading Scale to Students and Families

The scale isn't the problem. The explanation usually is.

By Clay Shumate3 min read

  2. What each level means, in plain language
  3. How to explain it before the first report card
  4. Helping students use the scale, not just receive it
  5. Common questions

A 1–4 grading scale makes sense to the teachers who use it. It often makes much less sense to the students and families who receive it. "She got a 3" can sound like a C to a parent raised on letter grades, when in most standards-based systems a 3 means the student has met the standard.

The scale itself isn't the problem. The explanation usually is. Here's how to explain a 1–4 scale so that students and families read it the way you mean it.

Why four levels instead of a hundred

Families usually ask this first, and it's worth having a clear answer. The education researcher Thomas Guskey has made the case plainly. On a 100-point scale, roughly the bottom 60 points describe different degrees of failure, which is a lot of precision spent on distinguishing kinds of failing. More levels also don't mean more accuracy: he cites studies in which different teachers scoring the same piece of work produced scores ranging from 50 to 96. His recommendation is an integer scale of about four levels, which is easier to apply consistently and makes recovery from a bad score realistic.1

The zero problem is the argument most families find convincing. On a percentage scale averaged over a grading period, one zero can take many perfect scores to recover from. On a 0–4 or 1–4 scale, a missed assignment hurts, but it doesn't bury a student.

What each level means, in plain language

LevelWhat it meansHow to say it to a family
4Goes beyond the standard"Applying it in ways we didn't directly teach."
3Meets the standard"Can do what the standard asks, independently. This is the goal."
2Approaching the standard"Has the basics, still needs support on the harder parts."
1Beginning"Just getting started. Needs significant help right now."

Local definitions vary, so match this to whatever your school or district uses. What matters is that the words are the same on your rubric, your gradebook and your letter home.

Say this early and often

A 3 is the target. A 4 is not "an A-plus." It's work that goes beyond what was asked. When families understand that, most of the confusion disappears.

How to explain it before the first report card

  1. Send a one-page explainer home early. Before any scores arrive, not after the first confused email.
  2. Use one real example. Show a piece of work at each level for the same standard. Families understand examples faster than definitions.
  3. Have students explain it. A student who can tell their family "I'm at a 2 on this standard because I can't yet do X" understands the scale, and so will the family.
  4. Be honest about conversion. If your school still reports a letter grade, show exactly how the 1–4 scores become that letter. Hidden math creates distrust.

Helping students use the scale, not just receive it

The scale works best when students can argue for their own level with evidence. Ask them to name the score they think they've earned on a standard and point to the work that proves it. That conversation teaches the standard better than a returned score ever will.

For the full breakdown, including why 3 is the target and what to do when your district requires percentages anyway, see this guide to the 1–4 grading scale from Elevate the Norm, which comes with a free PDF.

Common questions

Is a 3 the same as a B?

Not exactly. A 3 means the student meets the standard, which is the goal. If your school converts to letters, the conversion is a local decision. Share it openly rather than letting families guess.

Can a student earn a 4 on everything?

It's possible, but a 4 means going beyond the standard, so it should be relatively rare. That's by design, not a sign the scale is harsh.

What happens to late or missing work?

Many standards-based systems report behaviors like timeliness separately from mastery, so a late assignment doesn't disguise what a student actually knows. Check your school's policy and explain it plainly.
