Formative Assessment Without the Extra Grading

Done well, formative assessment should shrink your grading pile, not grow it.

By Clay Shumate3 min read

Formative assessment has a reputation problem. Teachers hear the phrase and picture another stack of papers to grade. That's backwards. Done well, formative assessment should reduce the grading load, because most of it shouldn't be graded at all.

The point of formative assessment is to find out what students understand while there's still time to do something about it. Here's how to get that information without adding hours to your week.

Why it's worth the effort

The case for formative assessment goes back to Paul Black and Dylan Wiliam's influential 1998 article "Inside the Black Box." Reviewing the research, they argued that formative assessment is an essential part of classroom work and that developing it can raise standards of achievement. They were equally clear that it takes sustained teacher development, not a quick fix.1

That second point matters. Formative assessment isn't a product you buy or a form you print. It's a habit of checking, noticing and adjusting.

Quick checks that take no grading

StrategyHow it worksTime
Exit ticketOne question at the end of class; sort answers into "got it," "almost" and "not yet"5 minutes to read
Whiteboard responseEvery student writes an answer and holds it up at onceInstant
Hinge questionA multiple-choice question where each wrong answer reveals a specific misconception2 minutes
Confidence checkStudents rate their confidence before and after a task1 minute
Walk and listenCirculate during group work with a clipboard and note three patternsDuring class

None of these need a grade. They need a decision.

The part most teachers skip: using the information

Collecting the information is the easy part. The value is in what you do next. Before you collect anything, decide what you'll do with each result:

  • Most students got it: Move on, and pull a small group for the few who didn't.
  • About half got it: Pair students up and have those who understand explain it.
  • Most students didn't get it: Reteach, and do it differently the second time.
The one rule

If nothing about tomorrow's lesson could change based on the answers, it isn't formative assessment. It's just a quiz.

Letting students use it too

Formative assessment isn't only for the teacher. When students see their own results against clear criteria, they can adjust too. A student who knows exactly which step they keep missing can fix it. A student who only sees "72%" usually can't. That's also why self-assessment and formative assessment tend to work best together.

A longer list to choose from

Variety helps. Students tune out when every check looks the same. For more options, including how to act on what you find, see this list of formative assessment strategies that work in real classrooms on Elevate the Norm.

Common questions

Should formative assessments count toward grades?

Generally, no. When they count, students focus on the score instead of the feedback, and they're less likely to show you what they don't understand.

How often should I check for understanding?

Informally, several times a class. More formal checks like exit tickets work well a few times a week, as long as you actually read them.

What if students just copy each other's whiteboard answers?

Have everyone hold them up at the same moment on a signal. It's a small routine change that makes copying much harder.
